The Nations Before the Map

Ready for your review Project: Growing Stone Library Batch: 21 (one batch) Created: 2026-10-20 Repo: Documents\LLL-Web

A new study page at /collections/nations-before-the-map/, built around the 1967 USGS National Atlas map of early tribes, culture areas and language families. It asks whose names are written on the map, how the nations name themselves, how their languages connect, and what the nations themselves tell about where they came from. North Carolina runs through it as the close-to-home thread.

01What we're building

A seventh study in the Library collections, in the same house style as Whose Name Is On the Gate? and Names on the Wall: breadcrumb, share row, numbered sections with anchors, four interactive widgets, study questions, a back link and a sources block. The page is static. It has no login, no forms, stores nothing about the reader, and makes no network calls (D-013).

The map labels many peoples by names outsiders gave them. The page puts each label beside the nation's own name, shows how language families cross culture areas, and gives the nations' own origin and migration accounts next to what linguists reconstruct, kept in separate lanes.

In plain termsA reading page with four panels you can tap to light things up. Following your site's rules, every word is written into the page itself, so it reads fine on bad signal, prints whole and works with scripts off. The seed files are the research record that the page is written from, and they're kept in the repo for review.

02Research findings

  • The site repo: Documents\LLL-Web (GitHub lll-web). Each study is collections/<slug>/ with index.html, app.js and styles.css. Last batch was 20; last decision D-023. .vercelignore serves only the root pages, collections/ and shared/, so docs/ stays private.
  • Site rules that shape this build: D-001 vanilla, no build step · D-012 every state in the HTML, JS only toggles · D-013 no network calls, no CDN · D-014 comparisons static, never tabbed · D-015 persistent nav from headings · D-016 patterns highlight, never swap · D-017/D-019 fit 836px at 1440×900 for simultaneity widgets · D-021 self-hosted fonts · D-023 sources line and ruled citations.
  • Existing solutions: no library (D-001, D-013). Regions are inline SVG <path> elements with click and keyboard handlers that add a highlight class, the same approach as the Inscription Stone on the Gate study.
  • Source map: Library of Congress item 95682185, "National atlas. Indian tribes, cultures & languages", William C. Sturtevant, USGS. The catalogue gives the printing as 1991 and citations give 1967. The LOC states its Geography and Map Division digitized collections are free to use and reuse; credit "Library of Congress, Geography and Map Division." Download through the item page or its IIIF manifest. The image ships inside the study folder (D-013).
  • The copy you shared has altered colors. Its fills do not match its own legend (the Great Basin is pink, which the legend calls Muskogean). Use only the LOC scan.
  • The mapmakers' own caveats (USGS publication 32595): borders are drawn from different centuries in different regions, and sharply drawn territories are misleading. Section VII quotes this in paraphrase.
  • Names research: 32 entries, 31 ready to publish after review, in names.seed.json. 30 of the 32 have at least one government that still officially uses the map's label (Cherokee Nation, Seminole Tribe, Navajo Nation, Oglala Sioux Tribe and others). Only Papago and Iroquois are no longer in any official name. The page must not suggest the old labels were dropped; the point is who chose the name.
  • Origins research: 13 Lane A entries from the nations' own sites, 8 Lane B language-family homelands, and a table of how the 1967 "stocks" stand today, in origins.seed.json and families.seed.json.
  • Respect guidance: the Protocols for Native American Archival Materials (endorsed by the Society of American Archivists, 2018); the Hopi Cultural Preservation Office's request that stories not be published without permission; the Delaware Nation's caution about publishing Lenape culture; seasonal storytelling for Diné and Ojibwe accounts; and Local Contexts TK labels as a model for the story badges.
  • Known fake: the Walam Olum is a 19th-century fabrication (Oestreicher 1994), and the Delaware Nation calls it fraudulent. It never appears as a source.
  • North Carolina: nine state-recognized tribes, with the Catawba Nation added in June 2026. The Lumbee Tribe received full federal recognition on 2025-12-18. These are recent changes, so Claude Code re-checks the NC Commission of Indian Affairs page on build day.
  • Anthropic/MCP shortcuts: none applicable. D-013 forbids them on Library pages.

06The four widgets

All four follow D-012: every word renders in the HTML, controls are hidden until JS runs (has-js), and JS only adds or removes a highlight class. Nothing is hidden, swapped or fetched, so copy, find, print and translation tools get the whole study. Each is labeled "Interactive · Widget N of 4", works by keyboard (Tab, Enter), and stacks to one column under 640px.

What it shows
One traced map carrying both layers at once: culture-area outlines as lines and language families as fills. Below it, a static family tree with every family as a branch, showing far-apart relatives. Example: Navajo and Apache sit with the Athabaskan peoples of Alaska and Canada.

11Content rules

  1. Lane A text is a short paraphrase, in our own words, of what the nation itself has published, and always links to that page. No outside retellings in Lane A.
  2. Never retell a story a nation marks restricted (Catawba). Show only that the Nation holds it.
  3. Winter stories (Diné, Anishinaabe) carry the "Winter story" badge and stay at outline length.
  4. Never cite the Walam Olum except to name it as a fabrication in Section VII.
  5. Never present a map label as rejected when the nation still uses it officially. The "Still used" badge comes from still_uses_label.
  6. Keep the lanes visually separate and never merge a tradition and a reconstruction into one sentence.
  7. Use present tense for nations: they are governments and communities today.
  8. No meanings for labels that a source marks as disputed (for example, do not state "Apache means enemy").
  9. Entries with publish: false are never written into the page.
  10. Researched citations reach the page only after Hashem rules on them (D-023). Where a source is known only second-hand, the CHANGELOG says so.
  11. Quotes from any source stay under 15 words; everything else is paraphrased.
  12. NC recognition facts are re-checked against the NC Commission of Indian Affairs page on build day.

18Why it's built this way

  • Two lanes, not one story. A nation's own account and a linguist's reconstruction are different kinds of claims. Side by side, each keeps its standing and the reader can compare them.
  • Only the nations' own published words in Lane A. Several nations ask that their stories not circulate out of context. Using only what they chose to publish respects that and keeps the page defensible.
  • A redrawn map instead of the shared copy. The shared copy's colors contradict its legend. The LOC scan is the honest original, and a traced overlay lets the widgets work without copying the error.
  • Every word in the page, not loaded later. Your D-012 and D-013: a reader on bad signal never sees an empty box, and the page prints whole. The seed files stay as the reviewed record behind it.
  • Highlights instead of filters and pickers. D-014 and D-016: hiding rows would break print, copy and find, and the reader would lose the comparison.
  • Rejected: a tabbed Names widget or a people picker (breaks D-014); runtime JSON (breaks D-012 and D-013); a web-map library (breaks D-001 and D-013); including the Walam Olum as "disputed" (it is a documented fabrication).
