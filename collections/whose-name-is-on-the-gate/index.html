Whose Name Is On the Gate?

Benefaction, Inscription, and the Twelve Men Who Could Never Have Paid

"And the wall of the city had twelve foundations, and in them the names of the twelve apostles of the Lamb." — Revelation 21:14

I · The Named Gate II · The Honor Economy III · A Donor Building IV · The Wrong Men V · The Inversion VI · Not Made With Hands

In the ancient world, a temple told you who paid for it. Names were cut into gates, pavements and architraves — and buried under foundations where no living eye would ever read them. It was an economy: money in, permanent honor out. Then a Galilean carpenter told twelve men who had no money that their names would be on the foundations of a city. This study asks what those twelve would have heard.

I

The Named Gate

A Real Man, A Real Door

Somewhere in the Temple complex the apostles walked through, there stood a set of bronze doors that everyone in Jerusalem called by one man's name: the Gate of Nicanor. The Mishnah records him. Josephus describes doors of Corinthian bronze more magnificent than the gold-plated ones around them. A legend grew up about his sea voyage from Alexandria with the doors lashed to the deck.

For centuries that was all anyone had — rabbinic memory and a story. Then in October 1902, a burial cave was opened on Mount Scopus. Inside was a limestone ossuary carrying an inscription in Greek, with the name repeated in Hebrew: these are the bones of Nicanor the Alexandrian, who made the doors.

No further explanation was thought necessary. The gift was so well known that "the doors" needed no other description — a whole city knew which doors, and whose.On the Nicanor ossuary, now in the British Museum

A man paid for a gate. The gate carried his name while he lived. His bone-box carried the same claim after he died. That is not the quirk of one wealthy Alexandrian — it is how the ancient world built sacred architecture, everywhere, for three thousand years.

The question this page follows

If the Twelve lived inside a world where names on sacred stone meant money, what did they hear when Jesus promised them exactly that — and told them, in the same breath, never to become the kind of men who buy it?

II

The Honor Economy

Euergetism, and the Names Under the Floor

The Greeks had a word for it. A wealthy man who funded public works was an euergetēs — a "benefactor," literally a good-doer — and the practice is called euergetism. Theatres, concert halls, libraries, baths, gymnasiums, fountains, markets: a patron funded it, and the building carried a line naming him, often closing with the Roman formula D.S.P.F. — de sua pecunia fecit, "done with his own money."

Four letters. The entire transaction.

But the practice is far older than Greece, and in its oldest form it is stranger. In Sumer and Babylon, kings buried inscribed clay cones, pegs and cylinders beneath the temples they built. Gudea of Lagash, who built or rebuilt fifteen temples at Girsu around 2100 BC, seeded them with cones shaped to look like a builder's nail — symbolically the nails holding the temple together, and a claim on divine protection for the ruler who put them there.

Nobody could read them. They were sealed in the ground. They were written for God, and for whoever dug there next.

Someone did dig. Nebuchadnezzar II records cutting a trench hunting for old foundation deposits, finding those of Naram-Sin — an ancestor separated from him by well over a thousand years — and then doing something remarkable. He did not remove the older inscription. He laid his own beside it.

Hold onto this

Gates advertise upward, to the city. Foundations testify downward, to God — and they hold layered names, predecessor and successor together, unseen. Remember which element Revelation gives the apostles.

III

A Donor Building

What Herod's Temple Said About Itself

The Temple complex the apostles walked through every festival was the largest benefaction project in the Jewish world. Herod the Great began the rebuild around the eighteenth year of his reign, employing a thousand priests as masons and carpenters so the holy precincts could be worked without defilement. Outbuildings and courts went on for another eighty years — still under construction during Jesus' ministry.

Herod himself bore the title Euergetes. So had Ptolemy III, Ptolemy VIII, Julius Caesar and Augustus. The scholar Adolf Deissmann observed that you could gather over a hundred inscribed instances of the title with very little effort. So when Jesus says the rulers of the Gentiles are called benefactors, he is not reaching for an obscure reference. He is naming the man who built the building.

Here is the sobering part. Almost nothing of the Temple's structures survives. The handful of significant inscribed fragments we do have includes a Hebrew stone reading "the place of the trumpeting," a Greek plaque recording a donation of flooring, and two Greek stones warning Gentiles not to pass a certain line.

Two of the few surviving voices from Herod's Temple are a donor's receipt and a boundary marker.What the stones themselves say

And a few hundred yards away, the Theodotus stone recorded a synagogue built by a man who listed his father's and grandfather's offices before he got around to the building. Scholars read him as a civic patron of standing. This is the world. Sacred space was funded, and funding was named.

A detail worth sitting with

In Acts 3, Peter and John meet a lame beggar at a temple gate and Peter says: silver and gold have I none. Whichever gate Luke means — and this is genuinely disputed — it stood in a complex where every significant gate had a price, and most had a patron.

IV

The Wrong Men

Why the Twelve Were Never Candidates

It would be sentimental to call the apostles destitute. Zebedee had hired servants (Mark 1:20). Matthew had capital and a tax franchise. Several ran a fishing operation with boats and nets. They were working men with assets.

But benefaction was not a matter of having savings. It was a matter of class. Look at who actually got named in first-century Jerusalem:

A Galilean fisherman funding a Temple gate was not a stretch. It was a category error. The nearest modern analogue is not "a working man could never afford a stadium naming right" — it is that the naming rights were never offered to him, because the honor system ran on standing he did not have and could not acquire.

And they knew it. That is the whole force of the argument they kept having. Luke 22:24 reports a dispute among them over which should be counted greatest. They were not debating theology. They were jockeying inside an honor economy they understood perfectly.

V

The Inversion

Forbidden the Title, Given the Stone

Into that argument, at the last meal, Jesus says two things in a row that only detonate when you hear them together.

First: the kings of the Gentiles lord it over them, and those in authority are called euergetai — benefactors. You are not to be that. The greatest becomes the youngest; the leader becomes the one who serves (Luke 22:25–26).

Then, without pausing: he assigns them a kingdom, and thrones (Luke 22:29–30).

You will not be benefactors — and you will have permanent names in the city.The sequence, compressed

The architectural vocabulary follows them everywhere after that. Solomon's two named pillars, Jachin and Boaz (1 Kings 7:21). James, Cephas and John, who "seemed to be pillars" — styloi (Galatians 2:9). The overcomer made a pillar in the temple with names written on him (Revelation 3:12). Built on the foundation of the apostles and prophets, Christ the chief cornerstone (Ephesians 2:20). Living stones (1 Peter 2:5).

In euergetism you pay for the stone and your name goes on it. Here the apostles are the stone, and the name is on them because of what they have become.

Now look at the city itself. Two walls, same architecture, opposite economies.

Who paid The Lamb. No sum appears anywhere in the passage.
The gates open outward toward Israel's inheritance; the foundations anchor downward in apostolic witness. John Gill, writing in the eighteenth century, read the apostolic names as an allusion to the old practice of inscribing builders' names on foundation stones in their memory. He was reading the same custom this page has been tracing.

One more echo, and it is not a small one. The Temple Scroll from Qumran (11Q19) — the longest of the Dead Sea Scrolls — lays out an ideal temple whose middle and outer courts each have twelve gates, one for every tribe, three to a side. The scroll even names them: Simeon, Levi and Judah east; Reuben, Joseph and Benjamin south; Issachar, Zebulun and Gad west; Dan, Naphtali and Asher north.

So twelve tribal gates was not John's invention. It was a live architectural idea in Second Temple Judaism before any apostle was born. What John adds is the course underneath.

VI

Not Made With Hands

The Charge at the Trial, and the Stone in Daniel

At Jesus' trial the witnesses bring a charge: he said he would destroy this temple made with hands, and in three days build another not made with hands (Mark 14:58).

Sit with what that phrase does to everything above. A building with no human builder can carry no de sua pecunia fecit. There is no patron to thank, no sum to record, no lineage to list first. The entire apparatus of sacred naming has nothing to attach to.

And yet the city has twelve names cut into its foundations.

Where this library gets its name

In Daniel 2, Nebuchadnezzar sees a statue of metals — and a stone cut out without hands strikes it, and becomes a mountain that fills the whole earth. The same logic exactly: no human hand, no human patron, and the thing still becomes the house. The Growing Stone is the unbought building.

It is worth noticing who that stone lands on. The statue is the honor economy in one image — gold at the head, and every metal in it the kind of thing a benefactor donates. Daniel's stone does not buy the statue. It does not out-give it. It breaks it, and grows.

Every temple in antiquity advertised who paid. The Twelve were the wrong class of men for that economy, and they knew it, and they argued about it at the table. Jesus forbade them the title on a Thursday night and handed them the stone anyway — and when John finally sees the city, the names are cut exactly where the ancient world put the names it never meant anyone to read.

Where the Ground Is Uncertain

Disputes This Study Does Not Settle

Some of the material above is firm and some is argued. It is worth knowing which is which before anyone repeats it.

The Beautiful GateNo ancient source outside Acts 3 mentions a gate by that name. Both the inner Nicanor Gate and the outer Shushan Gate are proposed. Nicanor is the common guess, not a settled identification.
Nicanor: donor or craftsman?The ossuary says he made the doors. Older reference works hedge on whether he paid for them, built them, or both.
Is it the same Nicanor?Klein (1920) was confident the ossuary man is the Temple-gate man. Schwartz (1991) raised doubts. Most treatments accept the identification; it is not unanimous.
When were the doors installed?Probably around the middle of the first century — possibly after Jesus' ministry. That does not weaken the argument here; it shows donor gates were still being installed in the apostles' own lifetime.
Dating the Theodotus stoneMost scholars place it before AD 70. A minority have argued for the second or third century.
Matching apostles to stonesScripture does not do it. Neither should we.
