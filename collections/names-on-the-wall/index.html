Names on the Wall: Three Faiths, One Street at Dura-Europos

I

One street, one wall, three houses

Around 256 CE, three houses of worship stood a short walk apart on the same street in Dura-Europos. One belonged to Jews, one to Christians, and one to Roman soldiers who served the god Mithras.

Dura-Europos was a small Roman garrison town on the Euphrates River, in what is now eastern Syria. It sat on the empire's edge, facing the Sasanian Persians across the desert.

When the Persians came, the town's defenders did something desperate. They packed sand and rubble against the inside of the western wall to make it thick enough to hold. The houses along that street were filled in and buried. The city fell anyway. But the sand kept those rooms, and the paint on their walls, hidden for almost 1,700 years.

When excavators uncovered them in the 1920s and 1930s, they found something no one expected: three rival faiths, side by side, each telling its story on its walls. And each one left a different answer to a simple question. Who signed the wall?

II

Three houses on one street

The synagogue was the boldest remodel. Its builders made a hall large for its town, with painted walls rising about seven meters. That was a public statement: we are here, and this is our story.

The house church barely changed the house at all. From the street it still looked like a home. Only one small room inside was set apart and painted.

The Mithraeum was built to feel like a cave, the place where Mithras killed the cosmic bull. Initiates reclined on the side benches for shared meals, facing the bright niche at the end of a dark room.

III

Who signed the wall

The three houses left three different kinds of names behind. One named its builders, one named its ranks, and one mostly asked God to remember.

The synagogue named its builders. Painted tiles in the ceiling carry dedications in Aramaic and Greek. They name the elder who led the rebuilding and the members who helped pay for it, and they ask that those givers be remembered. Giving was honorable, and it was recorded where the whole congregation could look up and see it.

The Mithraeum named its ranks. Its walls are crowded with inscriptions and graffiti from soldiers. Men recorded their names, their military posts, their gifts, and their progress through the cult's seven grades of initiation. The temple doubled as a network. A name on that wall said who you were and how high you had climbed.

The house church mostly left prayers. No dedication names a patron or a builder. What survives are a few short scratched lines asking Christ to remember a person by name. The name is there, but it is not credit. It is a request.

Set side by side, the walls ask the reader a question that still matters. When you give, whose name goes on the wall, and who is it written for?

IV

What the art was for

Each house painted for a different reason: the synagogue to remember a people's story, the church to explain a single act, and the Mithraeum to map the heavens.

The synagogue told history. Bands of scenes wrap the hall: baby Moses drawn from the Nile, the crossing of the Red Sea, Samuel anointing David, Elijah, and Esther before the king. The figures wear Roman and Persian clothing, as if the stories happened in Dura. God's face is never shown. Instead, a hand reaches down from heaven to guide and rescue. The paintings also overturn an old assumption that early synagogues never showed people at all.

The church explained baptism. Its paintings sit around the font, and they point to what happens in the water. Jesus heals a paralyzed man. Jesus and Peter walk on the water. Above the font stands the Good Shepherd carrying a sheep, a figure borrowed from common Roman art and given new meaning. One large panel shows women carrying torches toward a white building. Many read it as the women arriving at Jesus' tomb; others see the parable of the wise virgins. Scholars still debate it.

The Mithraeum mapped the cosmos. At its center, Mithras plunges a knife into a bull while a dog, a snake, a scorpion, and a raven gather around. The arch above carries the signs of the zodiac. The Sun and Moon look on. The art was not a story to follow. It was a picture of the universe, meant to be understood only by those who had been initiated.

The synagogue paintings are usually called frescoes, but they were painted in tempera on dry plaster.

V

The wall that saved them

The houses survived because a defense failed. The rampart built to save the city buried them instead, and that burial is why we can see them today.

The Rampart

Around 256–257 CE, the defenders filled the street along the western wall with packed earth and sand. Houses, temples, and the church on that street disappeared under the embankment. The Persians took the city, the people were gone, and the desert covered the rest.

The three sacred spaces sat in the path of the rampart. The walls that held their paintings were packed in and sealed from light and weather. Rooms farther from the wall fell to ruin. These were kept.

The story did not end in the ground. The synagogue panels went to Damascus in the 1930s. The church's baptistery and the Mithraeum's niche went to Yale. During the Syrian civil war, the Dura-Europos site itself was heavily looted. The paintings survived only because they had already been moved.

VI

For study and reflection

This page pairs with "Whose Name Is On the Gate?". That study looked at benefaction: givers whose names were carved on public buildings. Dura shows the same practice inside three houses of worship, and one house that set it aside.

  1. The synagogue honored its donors on the ceiling. The Mithraeum honored its soldiers on the walls. What did each community believe a name on the wall was for?
  2. The house church left prayers asking to be remembered, not dedications. How does that compare with Jesus' teaching to give in secret (Matthew 6:1–4)?
  3. The synagogue told a people's shared history. The church pointed to one act of rebirth. What does each suggest about how that community understood belonging?
  4. The Mithraeum was open only to initiated men. The synagogue was built for a whole congregation. The church hid inside a home. Which kind of room does your own community most resemble?
  5. A rampart meant to save the city failed, yet it preserved these rooms. Where have you seen something kept by the very thing that seemed to end it?
